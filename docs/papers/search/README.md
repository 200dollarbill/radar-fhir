## The short answer

The link you wanted strengthened is now much stronger, with one important catch.

**Stronger:** there is direct evidence that a JVP waveform feature predicts outcomes in heart failure. A Y descent deeper than the X descent was independently associated with cardiac events (HR 2.05 in 322 HFpEF patients, HR 2.68 in 350 HF patients). The first study got this from a non-invasive neck recording processed with signal processing, the closest match to your device. These papers also answer the thermometer objection, because the information is in the waveform shape from one recording.

**The catch:** that evidence is about wave shape and relative depth. The RADAR group's output is described as **time intervals**. These searches found no study linking JVP, carotid or IJV time intervals to outcomes. Ask the RADAR group whether their output can include X and Y descent depth, because your strongest clinical link depends on it.

## L4: does a single measurement have value?

**Yes as a risk marker, and the evidence is contested.** Single-time assessments predicted outcomes in large and small studies: Drazner 2001 (RR 1.32 for HF hospitalization, adjusted, SOLVD), Kasai 2023 (OR 5.06, pre-discharge), Ceelen 2025 (HR 1.69 at 180 days), Simon 2018 (HR 5.2 for 30-day readmission), Pellicori 2015 (HR 10.05 between tertiles, outpatients).

**Against:** Meyer 2009 found no intrinsic association after propensity matching (HR 0.94 for HF hospitalization). Sarma 2026 and Cendrós-Cámara 2026 also found no independent association in their settings. Parenti 2024 found no evidence of rater reliability for ultrasound. Selvaraj 2019 shows that change over time carries information too.

**How this helps you:** Meyer's own conclusion is that JVP is a marker of overall sickness. That supports your context argument: JVP reads best alongside comorbidity, labs and medication, which is what the integrated display provides. So the opposing paper can be used in your favour, as long as you present it fairly. All of this evidence comes from bedside or ultrasound assessment, so it does not show radar has the same value.

## L6: why radar is now the weakest link

- Radar JVP studies found are small and mostly healthy volunteers: 8 men (Suzuki), two volunteers (Arora), and one patient's earlier catheter data (Das).
- Non-radar methods have stronger validation, for example catheter comparison in 50 patients (Abnousi 2019) and invasive CVP correlation (Amelard 2021).
- **No radar study validated against ultrasound in patients was found in these searches.** Your group's planned USG validation at Soetomo would fill this gap, which is worth saying in the proposal, as long as that plan is confirmed.
