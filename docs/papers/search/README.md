## The short answer

The link you wanted strengthened is now stronger, with one important condition.

**JVP wave shape:** there is direct evidence that a waveform feature is associated with outcomes in heart failure. A Y descent deeper than the X descent was independently associated with events: HR 2.05 in 322 HFpEF patients (Harada 2019) and HR 3.15 in 387 HF patients (Harada 2023). HR 2.68 in 350 patients (Harada 2024) points the same way. In the 2019 study the recording was non-invasive: a contact transducer held on the neck, with signal processing and two blinded cardiologists judging the pattern. That is a surface recording like yours, but it is not contactless and it was not radar. These are observational studies from one group, and the cohorts may overlap.

**Carotid time intervals:** the RADAR group's output is described as time intervals, and here carotid evidence exists. Shorter systolic ejection time predicted mortality in 997 HFrEF patients (adjusted HR 1.06 per 10 ms), and ejection time from the carotid Doppler waveform had AUC 0.81 for EF under 40% (Polak 2015). The outcome studies measured it by echo, not from the carotid pulse, and the association held in HFrEF but not HFpEF.

**The condition:** no study linking **JVP or IJV** time intervals to outcomes was found, even with a search aimed at time intervals. So ask the RADAR group two things: can their output include X and Y descent depth, and which carotid intervals do they report? Your strongest clinical links depend on those answers.

**Claude's inference, not a finding from these papers:** this evidence offers an answer to the thermometer objection, because the information sits in waveform shape and timing from one recording, not only in a single value.

## L4: does a single measurement have value?

**Yes as a risk marker, and the evidence is contested.** Single-time assessments predicted outcomes in large and small studies: Drazner 2001 (RR 1.32 for HF hospitalization, adjusted, SOLVD), Kasai 2023 (OR 5.06, pre-discharge), Ceelen 2025 (HR 1.69 at 180 days), Simon 2018 (HR 5.2 for 30-day readmission), Pellicori 2015 (HR 10.05 between tertiles, outpatients).

**Against:** Meyer 2009 found no intrinsic association after propensity matching (HR 0.94 for HF hospitalization). Sarma 2026 (catheter CVP) and Cendrós 2026 also found no independent association in their settings. Parenti 2024 found no evidence of rater reliability for ultrasound. Selvaraj 2019 shows that change over time carries information too.

**How this helps you:** Meyer's own conclusion is that JVP is a marker of overall sickness. That supports your context argument: JVP reads best alongside comorbidity, labs and medication, which is what the integrated display provides. So the opposing paper can be used in your favour, as long as you present it fairly. All of this evidence comes from bedside, ultrasound or catheter assessment, so it does not show radar has the same value.

## L6: why radar is now the weakest link

- Radar JVP studies found are small and mostly healthy volunteers: 8 men (Suzuki), two volunteers (Arora), and one patient's earlier catheter data (Das).
- Non-radar methods have stronger validation, for example catheter comparison in 50 patients (Abnousi 2019) and invasive CVP correlation (Amelard 2021).
- **No radar study validated against ultrasound in patients was found in these searches.** Your group's planned USG validation at Soetomo would fill this gap, which is worth saying in the proposal, as long as that plan is confirmed.
